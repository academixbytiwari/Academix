Propositions and their Converses

Class 9 Mathematics · Ganita Manjari Part II · Chapter 9

← Home Concepts Exercise 9.1
CHAPTER OVERVIEW

What will you learn?

This chapter teaches us how to check mathematical statements, reverse their conditions, prove statements and disprove false claims using counterexamples.

Important: A statement and its converse must be checked separately. A true statement does not automatically mean its converse is true.

Concept and Formula Sheet

1. Proposition

A proposition is a statement that has a definite truth value: true or false.

Example: “Every square has four equal angles” is true.

2. Converse of a statement

Consider the statement: “If P is true, then Q is true.” Its converse reverses the condition and conclusion.

Original: If P, then Q.
Converse: If Q, then P.

Example:

  • Original: If a number is divisible by 6, then it is divisible by 3.
  • Converse: If a number is divisible by 3, then it is divisible by 6.

The original is true, but the converse is false. For example, 9 is divisible by 3 but not by 6.

3. Counterexample

A counterexample is one valid example that proves a general statement false.

Claim: Every even number is divisible by 4.
Counterexample: 6 is even, but 6 is not divisible by 4.

4. Important mathematical results

If x = y, then x + a = y + a.
If x = y, then x² = y². But x² = y² does not always mean x = y.
For real numbers, if x³ = y³, then x = y.
If a = m² and b = n², then ab = (mn)².
If n = 24k, then n is divisible by both 4 and 6.
If n = 60k, then n is divisible by both 5 and 12.
If a number is divisible by 3, its digit sum is divisible by 3, and vice versa.

5. Divisibility and factors

  • A square of a prime number has exactly three positive factors.
  • A product of two distinct prime numbers has exactly four positive factors.
  • If two numbers share a common factor, that factor also divides their difference.
  • To disprove a universal claim, one correct counterexample is enough.

Think and Reflect

1. Equal angles in a triangle

Result: If two angles of a triangle are equal, the sides opposite those angles are equal.

Proof: Draw an altitude from the vertex of the third angle to the opposite side. The two right triangles formed have equal corresponding angles and a common altitude. They are congruent, so the opposite sides are equal.

The statement is true.
2. Can both a proposition and its converse be false?

Yes.

Statement: If a positive integer is even, then it is a multiple of 3. This is false because 2 is even but not divisible by 3.

Converse: If a positive integer is a multiple of 3, then it is even. This is false because 3 is divisible by 3 but is odd.

Both statements are false.
3. Examples where the converse is false

Geometry: A square has equal angles, but a rectangle with unequal adjacent sides also has equal angles. Therefore, equal angles do not guarantee a square.

Number theory: A multiple of 6 is a multiple of 3, but 9 is a multiple of 3 and not of 6.

Equal areas: Two triangles can have the same area but different side lengths, so equal areas do not guarantee congruence.

EXERCISE 9.1

Step-by-Step Solutions

Solutions are organised by question number. The original textbook question statements are not reproduced.

Question 1 — Parallel lines and corresponding angles

Proposition: True. When parallel lines are cut by a transversal, corresponding angles are equal.

Converse: If corresponding angles are equal, the two lines are parallel.

Equal corresponding angles are the test for parallel lines.

Proposition: True. Converse: True.
Question 2 — Square and equal angles

Proposition: True. Every angle of a square is 90°.

Converse: If a quadrilateral has equal angles, it must be a square.

This is false. A rectangle measuring 6 cm by 4 cm has four equal angles but is not a square.

Proposition: True. Converse: False.
Question 3 — Isosceles triangle and angle bisectors

Proposition: If AB = AC, then IE = IF.

Since AB = AC, the base angles at B and C are equal. Their angle bisectors therefore make equal angles with BC. The resulting triangles around the incenter are congruent, giving IE = IF.

Converse: If IE = IF, then AB = AC.

This converse is false. A triangle with angles A = 60°, B = 90° and C = 30° provides a counterexample in the stated construction: IE = IF, although the base angles are unequal.

Proposition: True. Converse: False.
Question 4 — Adding the same number

Proposition: If x = y, then a + x = a + y.

Adding the same number to both sides of an equality preserves equality.

Converse: If a + x = a + y, subtract a from both sides. This gives x = y.

Proposition: True. Converse: True.
Question 5 — Product of perfect squares

Let a = m² and b = n².

Then ab = m²n² = (mn)², which is a perfect square.

Converse: If ab is a perfect square, both a and b must be perfect squares.

This is false: take a = 2 and b = 8. Their product is 16 = 4², but neither 2 nor 8 is a perfect square.

Proposition: True. Converse: False.
Question 6 — Equal numbers and their squares

If x = y, then x² = y². This is true because equal numbers have equal squares.

Converse: If x² = y², then x = y.

Counterexample: x = 2 and y = −2. Both squares equal 4, but the numbers are not equal.

Proposition: True. Converse: False.
Question 7 — Equal numbers and their cubes

If x = y, then x³ = y³. This is true.

Converse: If x³ = y³, then x = y.

For real numbers, each number has exactly one real cube root. Taking the cube root of both sides gives x = y.

Proposition: True. Converse: True.
Question 8 — Divisibility by 24

If n is divisible by 24, it is divisible by 4 and 6 because 24 = 4 × 6.

Converse: If n is divisible by both 4 and 6, it is divisible by 24.

Counterexample: 12 is divisible by 4 and 6 but not by 24.

Proposition: True. Converse: False.
Question 9 — Divisibility by 60

If n is divisible by 60, it is divisible by both 5 and 12.

Converse: If n is divisible by both 5 and 12, it is divisible by 60.

Since the least common multiple of 5 and 12 is 60, every common multiple of these numbers is divisible by 60.

Proposition: True. Converse: True.
Question 10 — A number with exactly three factors

If n is the square of a prime p, then n = p². Its positive factors are 1, p and p².

Converse: If a positive integer has exactly three positive factors, it must be the square of a prime.

For a number to have exactly three factors, its prime factorisation must be p² for a prime p.

Proposition: True. Converse: True.
Question 11 — Product of two distinct primes

If n = pq, where p and q are distinct primes, its positive factors are 1, p, q and pq. Hence it has exactly four factors.

Converse: Every number with exactly four factors must be a product of two distinct primes.

Counterexample: 8 has four positive factors—1, 2, 4 and 8—but it is not a product of two distinct primes.

Proposition: True. Converse: False.
Question 12 — Common factors of n and n + 3

If n and n + 3 have no common factor greater than 1, then n cannot be divisible by 3. Otherwise, 3 would divide both numbers.

Converse: If n is not divisible by 3, then n and n + 3 have no common factor greater than 1.

Any common factor divides their difference, (n + 3) − n = 3. The only positive factors of 3 are 1 and 3. Since n is not divisible by 3, the only common factor is 1.

Proposition: True. Converse: True.
Question 13 — Finding counterexamples to prime-number claims

(i) For 4n² + 1, take n = 4.

4(4²) + 1 = 65 = 5 × 13.

(ii) For n² + n + 11, take n = 10.

10² + 10 + 11 = 121 = 11 × 11.

(iii) For 4ⁿ + 3, take n = 4.

4⁴ + 3 = 259 = 7 × 37.

All three claims are false because each has a composite-number counterexample.
Question 14 — More counterexamples

(i) Take n = 11, which is prime.

2¹¹ − 1 = 2047 = 23 × 89, so the result is composite.

(ii) Take n = 6, which is even.

2⁶ + 1 = 65 = 5 × 13, so the result is composite.

Both claims are false.
Question 15 — Divisibility by 8

(i) If n is divisible by 8, write n = 8k.

Then n = 2(4k) and n = 4(2k). Therefore, n is divisible by both 2 and 4.

(ii) Checking divisibility by 2 and 4 is not enough to guarantee divisibility by 8.

Counterexample: 12 is divisible by 2 and 4 but not by 8.

The original statement is true, but its converse is false.
Question 16 — Divisibility by 3 and digit sum

Statement 1: If a number is divisible by 3, then the sum of its digits is divisible by 3.

Statement 2: If the sum of a number's digits is divisible by 3, then the number is divisible by 3.

Both statements are true and each is the converse of the other.

Example: For 123, the digit sum is 1 + 2 + 3 = 6. Since 6 is divisible by 3, 123 is also divisible by 3.

Both directions are true.
Question 17 — Quadrilaterals and equal diagonals

(i)(a) If every quadrilateral of type Q has equal diagonals, the two sticks used as diagonals must be equal in length.

(i)(b) The arrangement may matter. Equal diagonals alone do not guarantee every quadrilateral property. For example, a rectangle needs diagonals that bisect each other.

(ii)(a) If equal diagonals guarantee type Q, choosing equal sticks is sufficient to meet the stated condition.

(ii)(b) Any arrangement that forms a proper quadrilateral will satisfy the stated property, because its diagonals are equal. No additional condition is given in this direction.

Remember: a statement in one direction does not automatically prove its converse.

Quick Revision

  • Write the converse by reversing the condition and conclusion.
  • Check the original statement and the converse independently.
  • For a false statement, give a valid counterexample.
  • For a true statement, explain why it must hold in every allowed case.
  • Do not confuse “if P, then Q” with “if Q, then P”.
Study tip: In reasoning questions, write the truth value first, then give the proof or counterexample.
