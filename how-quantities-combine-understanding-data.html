CHAPTER REVISION

Key Concept

When different quantities are combined, each value must be given the correct weight. A large group, a larger mixture or a more important assessment component may have a greater influence on the final result.

Remember: These pages contain solution steps and final answers, not copied textbook question statements. Use the question numbers to match them with your book.

Formula Sheet

Simple average
Average = Sum of values ÷ Number of values
Weighted average
Weighted average = (w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + wₙxₙ) ÷ (w₁ + w₂ + … + wₙ)
Combined average of groups
(Average₁ × Group size₁ + Average₂ × Group size₂) ÷ Total group size
Percentage
Percentage = (Part ÷ Whole) × 100
Amount in a mixture
Amount of substance = Concentration ÷ 100 × Mixture quantity
Mixture concentration
Concentration = (Total amount of substance ÷ Total mixture quantity) × 100%
Share of a stacked bar
Percentage share = (Category quantity ÷ Bar total) × 100
Hours from a daily percentage
Hours = (Percentage ÷ 100) × 24

Think and Reflect — Solutions

Page 8 — Combining averages of different-sized groups

Senior trainees' total height = 1324 cm.

Junior trainees' total height = 448 cm.

Total height = 1324 + 448 = 1772 cm.

Total trainees = 8 + 3 = 11.

Combined average = 1772 ÷ 11 ≈ 161.09 cm.

A simple average of the two group averages gives the wrong result because the group sizes differ.

Page 26 — Stacked bars and 100% stacked bars

1. A stacked bar chart shows actual quantities and totals. A 100% stacked bar chart shows each category's percentage share.

2. Yes, a stacked bar can be converted into a 100% stacked bar if its parts can be read and its total is non-zero. Divide every part by the total and multiply by 100.

3. Percentages alone do not uniquely determine actual quantities. The original total is also required.

4. Stacked bars compare actual amounts; 100% stacked bars compare proportions. A larger percentage does not necessarily mean a larger actual amount.

Example: Parts 20, 30 and 50 make a total of 100, so the shares are 20%, 30% and 50%.
Page 27 — Daily time-use chart

1. Approximate sleep shares: children 38%, youth 34%, adults 32% and elderly people 37%. Children spend a larger share learning; elderly people have a larger share for leisure, social activities and travel.

2. Sleep hours: children 38% × 24 = 9.12 h; youth 34% × 24 = 8.16 h; adults 32% × 24 = 7.68 h; elderly people 37% × 24 = 8.88 h.

3. The learning share can be lower for ages 15–24 because this group includes people who have completed formal education and started working or taking on other responsibilities. The chart shows a difference but does not prove its cause.

4. Adults spend approximately 14% on paid work and 15% on unpaid work and care.

Paid work ≈ 3.36 hours; unpaid work and care ≈ 3.60 hours per day.
Page 28 — Grouping time strips

Yes. Each full strip represents 24 hours or 100% of a day. Joining the pieces for each activity creates a segment whose length represents that activity's percentage.

A 100% stacked bar keeps the total daily time but does not show when each activity happened.
Page 30 — Teacher activity chart

1. Another teacher's bars may look similar if their routine is similar, but they need not be identical.

2. Using all seven days can change the bars because Saturday and Sunday routines may differ from weekdays.

Seven-day average hours = (5 × Weekday average + Saturday hours + Sunday hours) ÷ 7

3. Similar bars could represent people with different jobs. A chart of body states alone cannot reliably identify someone's occupation.

Target concentration = 3/4 × 8% = 6%.

Let x litres of water be added. The spice amount stays at 0.8 L.

0.8 ÷ (10 + x) = 0.06.

0.8 = 0.6 + 0.06x, so x = 0.2 ÷ 0.06.

(i) Keerthi is ahead because her gain in agility has greater weight than her loss in strength.

(ii) Rashi = (60 × 4 + 65 × 5 + 70 × 6) ÷ 15 = 985 ÷ 15.

Question 6

Food average = (5 × 5 + 3 × 4 + 2 × 3) ÷ 10 = 4.3.

Ambience average = (4 × 4 + 5 × 3 + 1 × 2) ÷ 10 = 3.3.

Service average = (1 × 5 + 2 × 4 + 2 × 3 + 4 × 2 + 1 × 1) ÷ 10 = 2.8.

Combined rating = (4.3 × 6 + 3.3 × 5 + 2.8 × 4) ÷ 15.

Answer: 3.57 stars approximately.
Question 7

Let x be the number of males and y the number of females.

x + y = 60 and (16.5x + 13.8y) ÷ 60 = 14.925.

Substitute y = 60 − x:

16.5x + 13.8(60 − x) = 895.5.

2.7x = 67.5, so x = 25.

Answers: (i) option (a); (ii) 25 males and 35 females.

(iii) New female average = (35 × 13.8 + 15.2) ÷ 36.

Answer (iii): Approximately 13.84 kg.

(iv) Remaining male total = 25 × 16.5 − 16.9 − 16.1 = 379.5 kg.

Answer (iv): 379.5 ÷ 23 = 16.5 kg.

(v) New average after losing 1 kg = (379.5 − 1) ÷ 23.

Answer (v): Approximately 16.46 kg.
Question 8

(i) Mix 1 L at 34% salinity with 2 L at 0.001%.

Combined salinity = (34 × 1 + 0.001 × 2) ÷ 3.

Answer (i): Approximately 11.334%.

(ii) Let x litres of purified water at 0.01% be added to 1 L at 34%.

(34 + 0.01x) ÷ (1 + x) = 0.01 is not a dilution equation for purified water. The correct equation is (34 + 0.001x) ÷ (1 + x) = 0.01 when the added water has 0.001% salinity.

34 + 0.001x = 0.01 + 0.01x.

33.99 = 0.009x, so x ≈ 3776.67 L.

Answer (ii): Approximately 3776.67 L of water at 0.001% salinity.

(iii) A mixture cannot have a concentration lower than both starting concentrations. If both waters are more saline than the desired 0.001%, the target is impossible.

Answer (iii): Impossible with the given starting concentrations.

END-OF-CHAPTER EXERCISES

Worked Answers

Question 1 — Cricket run rate

(i) Total runs after two overs = 6 + 12 = 18.

Answer (i): 18 ÷ 2 = 9 runs per over.

(ii) Runs after 19 overs = 6 × 19 = 114. Add 12 runs: 126 runs in 20 overs.

Answer (ii): 126 ÷ 20 = 6.3 runs per over.
Question 2 — Average price of shuttlecocks

(i) Total prices collected = ₹917 + ₹493 + ₹766 + ₹897 + ₹990 = ₹4063.

Total observations = 6 + 4 + 5 + 4 + 4 = 23.

Answer (i): ₹4063 ÷ 23 ≈ ₹176.65.

(ii) Nylon total = ₹1429 for 13 prices; feather total = ₹2634 for 10 prices.

Combined average = (1429 + 2634) ÷ (13 + 10).

Answer (ii): ₹176.65 approximately. Both methods agree.
Question 3 — Average share price

(i) Original cost = 25 × ₹150 = ₹3750. New cost = 10 × ₹30 = ₹300.

Average = ₹4050 ÷ 35.

Answer (i): Approximately ₹115.71 per share.

(ii) Let x be the number of new shares.

(5 × 150 + 30x) ÷ (5 + x) = 70.

750 + 30x = 350 + 70x, so 400 = 40x.

Answer (ii): 10 shares.
Question 4 — Mean depth of a pool

Mean depth = (4 × 9 + 5 × 7 + 6 × 7 + 7 × 3 + 8 × 2) ÷ 30.

= (36 + 35 + 42 + 21 + 16) ÷ 30 = 150 ÷ 30.

Answer: 5 hastas.
Question 5 — New average gold price

Starting amount: 1 g at ₹15,000 per gram.

(i) Buy 1 g at ₹30,000: (15 + 30) ÷ 2 = 22.5.

Answer (i): ₹22,500/g.

(ii) Buy 2 g at ₹30,000: (15 + 60) ÷ 3 = 25.

Answer (ii): ₹25,000/g.

(iii) Buy 1 g at ₹10,000: (15 + 10) ÷ 2 = 12.5.

Answer (iii): ₹12,500/g.

(iv) Buy 10 g at ₹10,000: (15 + 100) ÷ 11 ≈ 10.4545.

Answer (iv): Approximately ₹10,454.55/g.

(v) Buy 0.5 g at ₹30,000: (15 + 15) ÷ 1.5 = 20.

Answer (v): ₹20,000/g.

(vi) Buy 0.5 g at ₹15,000: (15 + 7.5) ÷ 1.5 = 15.

Answer (vi): ₹15,000/g.
Question 6 — Doubling all weights

Original weighted average = (w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + …) ÷ (w₁ + w₂ + …).

After doubling every weight, the numerator and denominator both contain a common factor of 2. Cancelling this factor gives the original average.

Answer: The weighted average does not change.

Question 13 — Custom weighted rating project

Example: bus-travel experience with safety weight 4, punctuality weight 3, comfort weight 2 and cleanliness weight 1. Total weight = 10.

Overall rating = (4S + 3P + 2C + L) ÷ 10

For ten sample passengers, calculated individual ratings may be 4.3, 3.8, 4.7, 3.3, 3.6, 4.6, 4.4, 3.1, 4.0 and 4.3.

Total = 40.1. Overall average = 40.1 ÷ 10 = 4.01.

Answer: Sample overall rating = 4.01 out of 5. Label the data as a sample; use real or class-collected data for your project submission.

For the 100% stacked bar, count the 1-star to 5-star ratings for each aspect. With ten responses, each response represents 10%.

Final Revision Checklist

  • Multiply each average by its group size before combining.
  • For mixtures, calculate the actual amount of each substance first.
  • Convert scores with different maximum marks to percentages before weighting.
  • Stacked bars show actual quantities; 100% stacked bars show percentage shares.
  • Use the scale and labels on the original textbook figure for approximate chart readings.
  • Label sample or project data clearly and never present it as measured textbook data.
